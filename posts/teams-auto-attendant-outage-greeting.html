A pre-staged outage greeting for a Teams auto attendant

A disaster recovery plan asked three questions about the company's main phone number. Each one sounded like a five-minute answer, and each one needed a check first. Here's what the checks found, the holiday-dated-in-the-past trick that gives you an outage switch, and the honest answer on failover.

Ram Mudigina November 18, 2026 ~6 min read

A client's director of technology was updating their disaster recovery plan and sent three questions about the company's main toll-free number, which runs through a Teams auto attendant:

  1. Who administers it, and who can change its greeting?
  2. Can we have an outage greeting ready ahead of time, and what does switching it on involve?
  3. If Microsoft 365 goes down, does the number go down with it?

All three sound like five-minute answers. None of them should be answered from memory, because each one depends on how this tenant is set up.

01Check before you answer

Two read-only lookups changed what we told the client.

Who can change it. The auto attendant listed two authorized users. Both had left the company. That looks like a problem, but neither had a voice applications policy assigned, and the tenant only had the Global and Tag:Default policies, with every permission set to False. So in practice the listed users couldn't change anything, and nobody on the client side could either.

Being listed as an authorized user means nothing on its own. The TeamsVoiceApplicationsPolicy is what grants the permissions.

What the number actually is. The number type decides the whole failover answer, so look it up rather than assume it:

# Which resource account and number sit behind the auto attendant
(Get-CsAutoAttendant -Identity <aa-id>).ApplicationInstances |
  % { Get-CsOnlineApplicationInstance -Identity $_ | Select DisplayName, PhoneNumber }

# CallingPlan, OperatorConnect or DirectRouting?
Get-CsPhoneNumberAssignment -TelephoneNumber <+1888...> |
  Select TelephoneNumber, NumberType, PstnPartnerName, ActivationState

It came back CallingPlan, with Microsoft as the partner.

02The outage greeting: a holiday dated in the past

Teams has no outage switch, so we built one out of holiday call handling.

Holiday call handling takes priority over business hours whenever a holiday's date range covers the current time. That's the whole mechanism.

So the switch is a holiday called Outage, dated to a day that has already passed. It's linked to its own call flow, which plays the outage greeting and then hands off. Because the date is in the past, it never triggers. To turn it on, you move the date range to now. To turn it off, you move it back. It takes effect in minutes, and the normal business hours menu is never touched.

We kept one generic message rather than a greeting per scenario. Callers don't need to know whether it's the network, the servers or an application. They need to hear that there's a problem and what happens next:

"Thank you for calling. We're currently experiencing a technical issue and some services may be unavailable. Please leave a message and we'll get back to you as soon as possible."

For the "who can change it" part, the client's technology lead was added as an authorized user, with a new voice applications policy that allows greeting changes and nothing else: business hours, after hours and holiday greetings. Changing greetings this way needs no Teams Premium. Changing holiday dates as an authorized user does, so switching the outage holiday on stays with the admins.

03The honest answer on failover

A Calling Plan number lives in Microsoft's cloud. That cuts both ways.

We wrote that into the DR plan as it stands. The plan doesn't promise a fallback that doesn't exist, and it didn't turn into a pitch for re-architecting the line. If the client later wants carrier-side forwarding, that's a separate decision with its own cost.

The one-line model

A holiday dated in the past is an outage switch you can flip in minutes, but it only helps while Teams itself is up. Say both halves in the DR plan.

04Gotchas from the build

None of these are in the docs' happy path.

SymptomRoot causeFix
Every write cmdlet returned Access Denied, even after a reconnectTeams Administrator had just been activated through PIM and hadn't reached Teams yet; reads work without it, so a read proves nothingThe Teams admin center worked first. Or wait 10–15 minutes and reconnect in a fresh window
Backup and live menu look different when nothing changedA JSON backup stores enums as numbers: Tone1 = TransferCallToTarget becomes 1 = 2Compare both sides as JSON, not as printed values
Greeting text isn't quite what was approvedThe admin center tidies text-to-speech punctuationRead back the exact text if the wording matters to the client
# Default menu unchanged? Compare like for like.
$before = $bak.DefaultCallFlow.Menu.MenuOptions | ConvertTo-Json -Depth 10 -Compress
$now    = $aa.DefaultCallFlow.Menu.MenuOptions  | ConvertTo-Json -Depth 10 -Compress
$before -eq $now   # True = keys, actions and targets all match

05Validate, then call it

Six read-only checks, then one real phone call.

  1. Authorized users, and the policy each one actually has
  2. The policy's permissions: only the three greeting changes are True
  3. The holiday association is on the auto attendant
  4. The Outage schedule's date range is in the past, so it's off
  5. The outage call flow carries the approved greeting and action
  6. The default menu matches the pre-change backup

Then call the number and hear the normal greeting. That's the test that settles it.

Back up before you touch it

Export the auto attendant with Get-CsAutoAttendant | ConvertTo-Json -Depth 15 before the change. It's the only way check 6 has something to compare against, and the only record of how the live menu was wired if something goes wrong.
